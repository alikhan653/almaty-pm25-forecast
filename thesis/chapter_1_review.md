# 1. ANALYTICAL REVIEW

## 1.1. Air quality in Almaty and Kazakhstan

### 1.1.1. Topographic and meteorological drivers

Almaty occupies a basin at the northern foothills of the Trans-Ili Alatau mountain range (43.24°N, 76.89°E, elevation 700–900 m above sea level), with peaks rising to over 4,000 m immediately to the south and south-east. This asymmetric terrain configuration is the primary determinant of the city's air quality regime, and it operates through two interacting mechanisms: cold-air drainage and thermally forced boundary-layer collapse.

Cold-air drainage occurs because the dense, chilled air that accumulates overnight on the mountain slopes flows downslope by gravity and pools in the Almaty basin. The surrounding terrain to the north, where the city transitions to the Kazakhstani steppe, offers relatively unobstructed outflow, but the valley geometry slows horizontal ventilation considerably. The result is that cold surface-air masses can persist for 12–36 hours after the initial nocturnal drainage event, particularly when synoptic-scale anticyclonic conditions suppress vertical mixing over the entire region. During sustained inversion events recorded in the training dataset (2024-10-01 to 2026-04-15), boundary-layer heights (BLH) fall to as low as 10 m, against a summer mean of approximately 1,000–1,500 m and a training-window mean of 257 m. This extreme range, 10 m at the low end, 3,935 m at the peak of thermally active summer afternoons, is itself a quantitative signature of the inversion-dominated climate and is directly exploited by the BLH feature in the forecasting model.

Temperature inversions arise when the vertical temperature profile of the atmosphere is inverted relative to the standard lapse rate: the air aloft is warmer than the surface layer. Under such conditions, vertical convective mixing is suppressed because a parcel of surface air, if displaced upward, encounters air that is warmer and thus more buoyant, and it sinks back. In Almaty, radiation inversions develop on clear winter nights when the snow-covered surface loses heat rapidly through longwave emission, chilling the near-surface air. Surface-based inversions of 5–15 °C have been documented in regional meteorological records \cite{Tursumbayeva2022}. Elevated inversions also form when warm air advects from the south over the mountain range and overrides the cold pool in the basin. Both inversion types act as a lid that confines surface-emitted aerosols to the lower 50–200 m of the atmosphere, where the city's population lives and breathes.

The seasonal modulation of boundary-layer depth explains why Almaty's PM2.5 problem is almost entirely a winter phenomenon. In summer, strong solar heating destroys the nocturnal inversion by mid-morning and drives the convective boundary layer to 1,500–3,000 m, diluting surface emissions into a volume that is ten to twenty times larger than the winter surface layer. This dilution effect, independent of changes in emissions, is sufficient to reduce ambient PM2.5 concentrations from winter highs exceeding 100 μg/m³ to the 12–17 μg/m³ summer baseline observed in the present dataset. The seasonality of PM2.5 in Almaty is therefore not a pure reflection of emission seasonality; it is the product of emission intensity multiplied by an extremely variable dilution volume.

### 1.1.2. Emission sources and seasonal patterns

Three primary source categories drive Almaty's PM2.5 loadings: residential coal combustion, thermal power generation, and vehicle traffic. Their relative contributions shift with season and time of day, and their combined effect is amplified by the meteorological drivers described in §1.1.1.

Residential coal heating is the dominant wintertime source. Peri-urban and low-income districts on the city periphery, particularly in the eastern and western micro-districts, lack connection to the centralised district heating network. Households in these areas rely on individual coal-fired stoves or boilers, which operate at much lower combustion temperatures than utility-scale furnaces and therefore emit disproportionately high quantities of unburned carbonaceous particles, fine particulate matter, and black carbon. The heating season in Kazakhstan officially runs from approximately 15 October to 31 March, corresponding to the cold-temperature window when residential heating demand is greatest. This six-month window almost exactly coincides with the high-PM2.5 months in the dataset: the monthly mean of 80.7 μg/m³ in December 2025 and 74.2 μg/m³ in January 2026, against a summer baseline of 12–17 μg/m³, define a seasonal amplitude factor of approximately five. The sharpness of the heating-season transition, visible in the training time series as a rapid concentration increase in mid-October, motivated the inclusion of a binary heating-season indicator (1 from 15 October to 31 March, 0 otherwise) as a categorical feature in the model.

Thermal power generation contributes through Almaty's two combined-heat-and-power plants, CHP-2 (located in the city's western industrial zone) and CHP-3 (in the northern periphery). Both plants burn Karaganda-basin coal and emit PM2.5, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides from stacks whose effective injection height exceeds the inversion-layer ceiling under calm conditions. During inversion events, emissions that would normally disperse into the mixed layer instead accumulate near-surface after dry deposition and secondary aerosol formation. The plants collectively supply approximately 70% of the city's district heating load \cite{WorldBank2022almaty}, making them non-dispatchable during the heating season.

Vehicle traffic compounds the problem through a different diurnal cycle. Almaty's passenger car ownership rate is among the highest in Central Asia, and a substantial fraction of the fleet is older petrol and diesel vehicles with limited emissions control. Traffic-related PM2.5, primarily brake dust, tyre wear particles, and direct exhaust, peaks during morning (07:00–09:00 local time) and evening (17:00–19:00) commutes. This diurnal pattern overlaps with the weakest boundary-layer mixing of the day, creating a double-peaked hourly concentration signature that is reproduced in the lag feature structure of the model. The hour-of-day cyclical encoding captures this pattern without requiring explicit source attribution.

A secondary emission category relevant to summertime periods is dust resuspension. The Almaty basin is bordered to the north by semi-arid steppe, and dry southerly winds in spring carry mineral dust from bare soil surfaces. These dust events can briefly elevate PM2.5 to 40–60 μg/m³ even in months outside the heating season, producing short-duration spikes that are visible as outliers in the summer baseline series. Dust events are not the focus of the present model, but they introduce heteroscedastic residuals in the spring transition period (April–May) that affect the RMSE decomposition in §3.4.

### 1.1.3. Health impacts and regulatory context

The health consequences of sustained PM2.5 exposure at Almaty's observed concentrations are quantitatively severe. The World Health Organization's 2021 air quality guidelines set the 24-hour PM2.5 limit at 15 μg/m³ and the annual mean guideline at 5 μg/m³ \cite{who_aqg_2021}. During the heating season of 2025–26, the cross-sensor median PM2.5 in the present dataset exceeded the 24-hour guideline on the majority of winter days. At exposures above 35 μg/m³, the WHO epidemiological evidence base links each 10 μg/m³ increment in 24-hour PM2.5 to increases in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular hospital admissions, and respiratory emergency department visits \cite{WHO2013revihaap}. Almaty's approximately two million residents are exposed to these concentrations for roughly six months of each year.

Kazakhstan's regulatory framework for ambient air quality is established by the Environmental Code of the Republic of Kazakhstan, enacted in January 2021 (Экологический кодекс Республики Казахстан, 2021). The Code sets maximum permissible concentrations (MPC) for PM2.5, mandates environmental impact assessment for major emission sources, and establishes a framework for emission trading and environmental permits. The national AI strategy for 2021–2025 identifies environmental monitoring and smart city applications as priority domains for artificial intelligence deployment. Despite this policy context, there is no publicly accessible, near-real-time, locally calibrated short-term PM2.5 forecast for Almaty that is openly documented or independently reproducible. The present work directly addresses this deficit.

The gap between regulatory intent and operational reality has several causes. Kazhydromet's reference monitoring network, while technically credible, is too sparse to provide the neighbourhood-level spatial detail needed for public exposure guidance, and its data publication lag makes real-time decision support impossible (§1.2.1). The commercial forecast services that do exist for Almaty (§1.2.3) operate without published evaluation metrics. Public health interventions, school closures on high-pollution days, voluntary traffic restrictions, biomass burning bans, require actionable 6–24 hour forecasts that are not currently available from an open, auditable source.

## 1.2. Existing monitoring systems

### 1.2.1. Reference-grade monitoring

The formal air quality monitoring network in Almaty is operated by Kazhydromet, the national hydrometeorological and environmental monitoring service of the Republic of Kazakhstan. Reference-grade monitoring stations in this network use β-attenuation monitors (BAM) or tapered-element oscillating microbalance (TEOM) instruments that meet World Meteorological Organization (WMO) performance requirements for PM2.5 mass concentration measurement and provide readings traceable to gravimetric gravimetric standards. Both instrument types are widely accepted internationally as reference-equivalent methods: BAM instruments operate by drawing a particle-laden sample through a filter tape and measuring the attenuation of a beta-radiation beam, while TEOM instruments determine mass accumulation from the resonance frequency shift of a vibrating glass element. Both achieve measurement uncertainties of ±5–10% under typical operating conditions \cite{WMO2016GAW227}.

The Kazhydromet network is, however, sparse relative to the geographic and demographic scale of Almaty. Fewer than ten operational stations are distributed across a city area of approximately 682 km², yielding an average area per station above 68 km². For comparison, the US EPA NAAQS monitoring guidance recommends station spacing on the order of 10–20 km in urban areas with known emission hotspots, while population-exposure studies typically require even finer resolution to capture neighbourhood-scale gradients. At the observed station density, the Kazhydromet network cannot resolve intra-urban spatial gradients, identify emission hotspots, or provide the spatially aggregated city-scale concentration that would serve as a single-number target for a forecasting model.

Equally constraining for the purposes of the present work is the data accessibility limitation. Kazhydromet does not expose a public, machine-readable API for near-real-time hourly PM2.5 data. Data are published in periodic bulletins and annual reports, with lags of days to months between measurement and public release. This lag structure makes Kazhydromet data unsuitable as a training target or real-time forecast anchor for an hourly prediction system. The present study therefore does not use Kazhydromet data as a primary input, though a future co-location study comparing the OpenAQ LCS median against Kazhydromet reference readings would substantially strengthen the quantitative interpretation of model results.

### 1.2.2. Low-cost sensor networks

The community low-cost sensor (LCS) network aggregated through the OpenAQ v3 platform substantially densifies the monitoring coverage available to researchers and policymakers in Almaty \cite{openaq_platform}. As of the API snapshot taken on 20 April 2026, 192 active monitoring locations within 25 km of the city centre (43.24°N, 76.89°E) report PM2.5 data through the OpenAQ platform, providing a spatial density roughly twenty times greater than the Kazhydromet reference network.

The dominant contributor to this network is the Almaty Air Initiative, a citizen-science organisation that has deployed AirGradient ONE open-hardware monitors across residential districts since 2024. AirGradient units account for 153 of the 192 stations (79.7%). AirGradient ONE is an open-source, community-built sensor platform based on the Plantower PMS5003 optical particle counter, whose design schematics and firmware are publicly available under an open-hardware licence. The Clarity Node-S, a commercial LCS device manufactured by Clarity Movement Co. (Berkeley, CA), contributes 38 stations (19.8%). A single AirNow-affiliated station provides the remaining 0.5% of the network. All data transmitted through OpenAQ are licenced under CC BY 4.0, permitting unrestricted research use with attribution. This open-licence condition is a prerequisite for the reproducibility objective of the present work.

The OpenAQ platform's v3 API provides a standardised JSON interface through which sensor readings, metadata, and quality flags can be queried programmatically. The ingest pipeline described in §2.2 issues hourly requests to the `/v3/locations/{id}/measurements` endpoint, filters readings by the plausibility criteria defined in §2.3, and assembles the city-wide PM2.5 median used as the model target. The combination of platform standardisation and open licensing makes OpenAQ the only viable data source for a fully reproducible Almaty forecasting pipeline as of the study period.

### 1.2.3. Commercial aggregators and the data gap

Commercial air quality platforms, notably IQAir and the AirKz aggregator, publish AQI dashboards and, in some cases, AI-based short-term forecasts for Almaty. IQAir's World Air Quality Report (IQAir, 2024) ranked Almaty among the most polluted cities in Central Asia, citing annual mean PM2.5 concentrations that are consistent with the seasonal patterns documented in the present dataset. These commercial platforms play a valuable role in public communication and policy advocacy.

Their limitation for scientific purposes is fundamental: neither IQAir nor AirKz publishes methodology documentation, calibration procedures, model architecture descriptions, evaluation metrics, or the underlying training data for their forecasting products. The IQAir forecast pipeline, in particular, ingests data from a global network of IQAir AirVisual Pro sensors and applies undisclosed correction algorithms whose performance has not been independently peer-reviewed. Without access to methodology, forecast skill cannot be independently assessed. Without access to training data, the published rankings cannot be reproduced. Without published evaluation against held-out data, any claimed accuracy is unverifiable.

This opacity creates a specific gap that the present work addresses: Almaty lacks a published, open, and reproducible short-term PM2.5 forecast whose inputs, processing steps, model architecture, and evaluation results are fully documented. The value of the present system lies not only in its forecast accuracy, though §3.2 demonstrates a 41% RMSE improvement over the CAMS global baseline at the 6-hour horizon, but in the fact that every step of the pipeline, from API ingest to Streamlit deployment, is version-controlled and replicable by any researcher with access to the public OpenAQ and Open-Meteo APIs.

The reproducibility requirement is not merely an academic formality. A forecast system that informs public health decisions must be auditable: a regulator, a public health official, or a peer reviewer must be able to trace any individual forecast back to its training data, feature specification, and model parameters. Closed commercial pipelines cannot meet this standard. The present work addresses it by publishing the complete pipeline source code, the BibTeX-documented data provenance, and a held-out test set evaluation on 2,311 hours of winter 2025–26 data that was excluded from all training and hyperparameter selection steps. Any user of the open-source repository can reproduce the RMSE = 23.44 μg/m³ result at the 6-hour horizon from scratch using only freely available data and a standard Python environment.

## 1.3. Low-cost sensors: capabilities and limitations

### 1.3.1. Operating principles and cost structure

Low-cost sensors (LCS) for PM2.5 measurement exploit optical particle counting through laser-scattering. An aerosol sample is drawn through a sensing chamber where a laser diode, typically operating at 780 nm wavelength, illuminates the particle stream. Individual particles or clusters scatter the laser light at angles and intensities that depend on their size and refractive index; a photodetector positioned at a defined scattering angle converts this optical signal to a count distribution across particle-size bins. The sensor firmware then applies a lognormal conversion model to estimate PM2.5 mass concentration from the particle-count data.

The two dominant LCS platforms in the Almaty network exemplify the diversity within this operating principle. The Plantower PMS5003, embedded in the AirGradient ONE, draws a sample flow of approximately 100 mL/min through a 34 mm × 20 mm sensing volume and outputs concentration estimates for PM1.0, PM2.5, and PM10 at one-minute intervals via serial UART protocol. The Sensirion SPS30, an alternative optical counter used in some AirGradient ONE units, adds a laser-particle-interaction cleaning cycle that reduces particle deposition on the sensing optics, extending the calibrated service life from approximately 8,000 to 12,000 operating hours \cite{sensirion2023sps30}. The Clarity Node-S employs a proprietary laser-scattering geometry with firmware-level humidity correction, though the correction algorithm is not publicly documented.

The economic case for deploying LCS networks rather than reference instruments is straightforward at these cost levels. Reference-grade BAM and TEOM instruments cost USD 20,000–80,000 per unit, plus installation, calibration, and maintenance costs that can approach the capital cost over a five-year operational period. In contrast, an AirGradient ONE sensor, including enclosure, microcontroller, and connectivity, costs approximately USD 100–150 in component form, and the Clarity Node-S retails for approximately USD 300–400. This 100–500× cost differential makes the 192-station Almaty network economically feasible for a citizen-science organisation, whereas a reference-grade network of comparable density would cost tens of millions of dollars. The trade-off is that LCS measurements lack the metrological traceability of reference instruments and are subject to the biases described in §1.3.2.

### 1.3.2. Humidity and drift biases

The primary known systematic bias for optical LCS in humid environments is hygroscopic growth of aerosol particles. Above approximately 70% relative humidity (RH), water molecules condense onto the surfaces of hygroscopic aerosol constituents, primarily sulfate, nitrate, and organic carbon species, enlarging the apparent particle diameter. Because LCS firmware translates particle diameter to mass concentration using refractive indices and density assumptions calibrated for dry particles, the measured PM2.5 is systematically inflated under humid conditions. Published intercomparisons of Plantower-class sensors against reference BAM instruments in urban environments report positive humidity biases of 20–60% at RH > 80%, with the magnitude depending on the hygroscopic fraction of the local aerosol \cite{DiAntonio2018humidity} \cite{Kosmopoulos2020}. During Almaty's winter inversion episodes, precisely the high-concentration events of greatest interest for forecasting, ambient RH frequently exceeds 75–85%, meaning that the LCS readings are likely to be positively biased during the most critical measurement periods.

Inter-unit variability is a second quantifiable source of uncertainty. Factory-calibrated units of the same model show concentration offsets of 10–30% under identical controlled-chamber conditions, attributable to tolerances in laser alignment, photodetector response curves, and firmware parameter sets \cite{Popoola2016electrochemical}. When units are deployed across a city with diverse microclimates, varying heights above ground (some AirGradient units are mounted at 1.5 m, others at 3–5 m on balconies or rooftops), and different local source influences, the inter-unit spread across the 192 Almaty stations during any given hour substantially exceeds the controlled-chamber 10–30% figure. The standard deviation across all active stations in the present dataset can exceed 30 μg/m³ in a single hour during peak winter episodes, confirming that individual-station readings are not representative of city-wide exposure.

A third bias source is temporal drift: the gradual degradation of laser intensity and photodetector sensitivity over months of field deployment. Laboratory studies of Plantower PMS5003 units subjected to accelerated aging report signal attenuation of 10–25% over approximately 8,000–10,000 operating hours \cite{Sayahi2019}. The training window in the present study spans 562 days (2024-10-01 to 2026-04-15), or roughly 13,500 operating hours for sensors that were already deployed before the training window opened. Systematic drift cannot therefore be excluded, particularly for AirGradient ONE units deployed in the inaugural 2024 network expansion. Without periodic reference co-location, the magnitude of drift in the Almaty network is not directly measurable from the available data.

No co-location calibration against reference instruments was performed in the present study. The PM2.5 values derived from the OpenAQ network should therefore be understood as relative concentration signals that track true PM2.5 variability but may carry systematic biases of ±20–50% in absolute magnitude during high-RH winter conditions. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged in the scope discussion (§4.2) and does not invalidate the relative accuracy comparisons among models trained and evaluated on the same LCS-derived target series.

### 1.3.3. Mitigation strategies applied in this study

The standard mitigation for inter-unit variability in a multi-sensor LCS network is spatial aggregation: combining readings from multiple co-located or nearby sensors so that random unit-specific offsets cancel in the aggregate. The present study applies this principle at city scale by computing the cross-sectional median PM2.5 across all stations with plausible readings in a given hour. The median, rather than the mean, is chosen because the distribution of station readings in any given hour is typically right-skewed, a small number of stations near localised sources (a coal stove, a road intersection) read substantially above the bulk of the network, and those outliers would inflate the mean disproportionately. The median is resistant to such outliers by construction.

Three additional constraints bound the aggregation. A minimum of three contributing stations per hour is required; hours with fewer than three readings are recorded as missing rather than propagated as unreliable estimates. A plausibility filter excludes readings outside the range 0–500 μg/m³, rejecting physically implausible values likely caused by sensor malfunction or communication errors. A further check discards readings from any station whose 24-hour running mean deviates by more than three standard deviations from the network median, flagging potentially faulty or improperly calibrated units. These thresholds were chosen based on the empirical distribution of the raw API data and are consistent with practices reported in multi-city LCS network studies \cite{morawska2018lowcost}.

The city-median aggregation procedure does not correct for humidity bias, which is common-mode across the network (all sensors experience the same ambient RH) and therefore does not cancel in the median. This means that the model target series is systematically inflated during high-RH winter episodes relative to the true gravimetric PM2.5. As a consequence, the forecast bias statistics reported in §3.2 reflect errors relative to the LCS-derived target, not relative to reference-grade measurements. This distinction is noted wherever comparative claims are made.

The scientific justification for proceeding with LCS data despite these limitations is that the alternative is no hourly, city-scale, open dataset for Almaty at any quality level. Reference monitoring exists but is not openly accessible in real time. Commercial platforms exist but are closed. The LCS network, with its known limitations managed by the aggregation and filtering procedures described above, is the only viable basis for a reproducible, operationally testable forecasting system. This position is consistent with the broader field consensus that LCS networks, used with appropriate quality controls, extend the reach of air quality science into data-sparse regions where reference monitoring cannot be economically deployed \cite{snyder2013paradigm}.

## 1.4. Air quality forecasting methods

Short-term PM2.5 forecasting methods span five broad methodological families, each with distinct data requirements, theoretical assumptions, computational demands, and performance characteristics. The selection among them is not merely a question of which method achieves the lowest benchmark error; it depends on the specific characteristics of the target problem, dataset size, feature availability, deployment constraints, and the physical regime driving concentration variability. This section reviews each family and builds to an explicit rationale for the method selection in §1.4.6.

### 1.4.1. Statistical and persistence methods

The simplest forecast for PM2.5 at horizon h is persistence: the predicted concentration at time t + h is set equal to the observed concentration at time t. Despite its trivial construction, persistence is a surprisingly competitive baseline for air pollutants at short horizons because PM2.5 exhibits strong positive autocorrelation at lags of one to six hours, driven by the relatively slow dynamics of boundary-layer mixing and emission accumulation. At the one-hour horizon, persistence routinely achieves R² values of 0.85–0.95 in urban sites with continuous emissions \cite{mahajan2018shortterm}. At the 24-hour horizon, persistence degrades substantially because inversion onset and clearing events decouple the current concentration from the next-day value.

Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models extend persistence by fitting a parametric autocorrelation and moving-average structure to the observed time series. The Box-Jenkins ARIMA(p,d,q) framework allows the inclusion of seasonal differencing terms, an SARIMA extension, which can capture the diurnal periodicity of PM2.5. When meteorological covariates are added as exogenous regressors, the model is termed ARIMAX or, in state-space form, a dynamic regression model. These models have well-understood statistical properties, produce interpretable coefficients, and require minimal computational resources to fit and forecast. Published applications to urban PM2.5 forecasting in moderate-pollution cities report RMSE reductions of 15–25% relative to naive persistence at the 24-hour horizon \cite{mahajan2018shortterm}.

The fundamental limitation of ARIMA-class models for the Almaty setting is their linearity. The onset of a temperature inversion is a non-linear threshold event: boundary-layer height can collapse from 800 m to 50 m within two to four hours as a function of synoptic pressure pattern, surface energy balance, and cold-air drainage dynamics. The relationship between BLH and PM2.5 concentration is strongly non-linear, a halving of BLH roughly doubles surface concentration if emissions are held constant, and the emissions themselves are non-linearly dependent on temperature (colder temperatures increase residential heating demand). ARIMA cannot represent these interaction effects without explicit interaction-term specification that would require accurate physical knowledge of the inversion onset mechanism. Kalman filter methods, which model the concentration as a latent state evolving through a linear Gaussian process, share this linearity constraint, though extended and unscented variants can accommodate mild non-linearities at the cost of increased tuning complexity.

### 1.4.2. Classical machine learning on tabular features

Gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) ensembles and random forests constitute the dominant paradigm in operational short-term urban AQ forecasting as of the present study period. These methods work by training an ensemble of decision trees on a tabular feature matrix whose columns include autoregressive lags of PM2.5, meteorological variables, and temporal encodings such as hour-of-day, day-of-week, and heating-season indicators. Each tree in the ensemble learns a non-linear partition of the feature space that explains residual variance not captured by the preceding trees. The gradient-boosting optimisation framework, introduced in the XGBoost formulation by Chen and Guestrin (2016) \cite{chen2016xgboost} and subsequently refined in LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, adds L1 and L2 regularisation terms to the tree-growing objective, which substantially reduces overfitting on moderately sized datasets.

Published comparisons of GBDT-class models against ARIMA and persistence baselines on urban PM2.5 data consistently report RMSE improvements of 20–40% at 24-hour horizons \cite{enebish2021ulaanbaatar, menares2021forecasting}. The improvement is most pronounced in cities with strong meteorological forcing, cold-season inversions, monsoon transitions, dust events, because the tree-splitting mechanism can capture the non-linear meteorology-concentration interactions that linear models cannot. For the Almaty setting, the inversion-driven concentration regime described in §1.1.1 places this problem squarely in the category where GBDT methods offer the largest gains over statistical alternatives.

A key design decision in GBDT-based forecasting is the construction of the lag feature set. For a forecast horizon of h hours, the model cannot use observations from time t + 1 through t + h because these are unknown at forecast time. The available autoregressive inputs are therefore observations at lags 1, 2, …, L beyond the earliest available observation, where L is a hyperparameter chosen to balance memory depth against feature dimensionality. The present study uses lags at 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours for PM2.5 and selects meteorological features at forecast-time (using Open-Meteo numerical weather prediction output for the future window) \cite{zippenfenig_openmeteo_2023}. This lag structure was determined by cross-correlation analysis of the training series and is described in detail in §2.4.

Random forests, the bagged-tree precursor to gradient boosting, are less commonly reported as primary models in recent urban AQ literature, having been largely superseded by XGBoost and LightGBM in competitive benchmarks. They remain valuable as ablation baselines because they share the tree-ensemble architecture with GBDT but use averaging rather than gradient optimisation, making their performance differential against XGBoost a direct measure of the benefit from boosting regularisation.

### 1.4.3. Deep learning approaches

Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and their gated recurrent unit (GRU) variants extend the sequence modelling capability of standard recurrent neural networks (RNNs) by introducing gating mechanisms, input, forget, and output gates in LSTM; update and reset gates in GRU, that regulate the flow of information through the hidden state over time. These gates allow the network to retain relevant information across sequences of hundreds to thousands of time steps without the vanishing-gradient pathology that limits plain RNNs. Applied to air quality forecasting, LSTM models replace the hand-crafted lag features of GBDT approaches with learned temporal dependencies, potentially capturing multi-scale autocorrelation structures that manual lag selection misses.

Published applications of LSTM to urban PM2.5 forecasting report competitive performance at 24–72 hour horizons when training sets exceed approximately 20,000–50,000 time steps \cite{qi2019pm25lstm}. At this scale, the capacity of the LSTM to learn non-linear temporal patterns appears to justify its additional complexity relative to GBDT models. However, across a wider survey of urban AQ forecasting studies, the reported gains of LSTM over gradient-boosting are inconsistent: approximately half of published comparisons find LSTM superior, and half find GBDT competitive or better, with the outcome depending strongly on dataset size, number of monitoring stations, and the degree of feature engineering applied to the GBDT competitor \cite{lim2021survey}. The inconsistency suggests that much of the reported LSTM advantage in favourable studies arises from GBDT underfitting due to insufficient lag features rather than from a fundamental architectural advantage of recurrent networks.

Convolutional-LSTM (Conv-LSTM) and CNN-LSTM hybrid architectures have been proposed for air quality settings with dense spatial monitoring networks, where the convolutional layers extract spatial concentration patterns and the LSTM layers model their temporal evolution \cite{Ding2021}. These methods require the monitoring network to have consistent spatial coverage, ideally a regular grid, because the convolution operation is defined over a spatially regular input. The 192 Almaty LCS stations are distributed unevenly, with high density in central residential districts and sparse coverage in industrial and peri-urban areas. Interpolating these stations onto a regular grid would introduce kriging or bilinear-interpolation artefacts, and the resulting synthetic spatial fields would not carry independent information beyond the city-median series already used as the model target. Spatially explicit deep learning is therefore not applicable to the present dataset without additional investment in network spatial design.

Transformer-based models, following the self-attention mechanism of Vaswani et al. (2017) \cite{vaswani2017attention}, have been adapted for time-series forecasting under the PatchTST, Informer, and Autoformer architectures. These models handle long-range dependencies through attention rather than recurrence and have demonstrated competitive performance on long-horizon (168–720 step) forecasting benchmarks. For the short-horizon PM2.5 task considered here, 6, 12, and 24 hours, the self-attention mechanism's advantage over GBDT lag features is marginal, because the relevant dependencies are contained within a 24–48 hour window that GBDT can represent compactly with a moderate-length lag vector. Transformer models also require substantially more training data than the approximately 9,200 hourly rows available in the present study; typical transformer time-series benchmarks use 10,000–100,000+ training instances per target series to prevent overfitting of the large parameter count.

The deployment context additionally constrains model selection. The present study targets a Streamlit application running on a free-tier hosting environment with CPU-only inference. LSTM and transformer models require either a GPU for efficient inference or a model compression step (ONNX export, TensorFlow Lite) that adds pipeline complexity. XGBoost models serialised as 2 MB Joblib files produce forecasts in under 20 milliseconds on a single CPU core, satisfying the latency requirement without compression or GPU acceleration.

### 1.4.4. Physics-informed and hybrid methods

Chemistry-transport models (CTMs) simulate atmospheric concentrations by solving the advection-diffusion-reaction equations governing the transport, chemical transformation, and deposition of pollutants in a discretised atmospheric domain. The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS), operated by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), provides freely accessible global hourly PM2.5 analyses and forecasts at approximately 40 km horizontal resolution and 60 vertical levels \cite{inness2019cams}. WRF-Chem and CMAQ are regional CTMs that can be configured at 1–12 km resolution with user-supplied meteorological boundary conditions and emission inventories; they are widely used in North American and European urban forecasting applications.

The fundamental limitation of CTMs for the Almaty use case is horizontal resolution. The city occupies a basin approximately 20–25 km across, and the inversion-layer dynamics described in §1.1.1 operate at spatial scales of 1–5 km. A 40 km CAMS grid cell covers the entire city and averages over the basin-mountain contrast that drives cold-air pooling. Sub-grid processes, particularly the nocturnal drainage flow from the Trans-Ili Alatau and the spatial heterogeneity of residential coal heating, are parameterised rather than resolved. The quantitative consequence is visible in the present study's results: CAMS achieves R² ≤ −0.18 on the Almaty test set at all three forecast horizons, meaning that the CAMS output is less correlated with the observed PM2.5 series than a constant prediction equal to the training mean. This negative R² is not primarily a calibration failure; it reflects the model's inability to predict the inversion-driven concentration ramps that dominate the Almaty winter record at any grid resolution.

Statistical post-processing and bias-correction methods can improve CTM output by training a correction layer on paired CTM-versus-observation data. Quantile mapping, additive bias correction, and linear regression on CTM output have all been applied to urban PM2.5 downscaling \cite{Wang2016CTM}. These methods improve the long-term mean bias but typically do not correct the timing errors in inversion-onset prediction, because the CTM misplaces the event in time, not merely in magnitude. Without accurate representation of the boundary-layer collapse dynamics, post-processing cannot reconstruct the correct hourly PM2.5 trajectory during onset events.

The present study uses CAMS output as an external naive baseline in the model evaluation (§3.2) rather than as a component of the trained models. This choice reflects both the negative R² demonstrated above and the principle that a forecast system should be evaluable on its own terms, without depending on computationally expensive CTM infrastructure that is not available to all potential users of the open-source pipeline.

### 1.4.5. Spatial methods

Spatial interpolation methods, ordinary kriging, universal kriging, inverse-distance weighting (IDW), and radial basis function interpolation, estimate concentration at unmonitored locations from observations at monitored locations by modelling the spatial covariance structure of the concentration field. Kriging in particular produces optimal linear unbiased estimates under the assumption that the concentration field is a second-order stationary random field with a parametric variogram model. These methods are most valuable when the primary scientific objective is mapping the spatial distribution of concentration across a domain, rather than predicting the temporal evolution of concentration at a fixed location.

The 192-station Almaty network provides sufficient spatial density to support variogram estimation and kriging in principle. However, the present study does not pursue spatial forecasting for two reasons. First, the uneven spatial coverage, high density in central residential districts, sparse in industrial and peri-urban zones, violates the spatial stationarity assumption required for well-conditioned variogram estimation. Kriging predictions in the undersampled zones would have confidence intervals that span the full range of observed concentrations, rendering them uninformative. Second, the operational objective of the present work is temporal forecasting: predicting the city-wide PM2.5 level 6–24 hours in advance, not mapping its spatial distribution at a specific time. A city-median aggregation of 192 stations already provides a representative single-number target, and spatial disaggregation of model forecasts is a scope extension that would require separate validation against the spatial reference-monitoring network that does not currently exist.

IDW and simpler deterministic interpolants are also noted here for completeness. These methods have no free parameters (beyond the distance-decay exponent) and are computationally trivial, but they are consistently outperformed by kriging in benchmark spatial studies \cite{Wong2004Interpolation}. In one published evaluation of seven interpolation methods applied to a 40-station urban network, kriging reduced the cross-validation RMSE by 18–25% relative to IDW \cite{Wong2004Interpolation}. The gains are insufficient to justify spatial modelling in the present context, where the temporal forecasting objective and the uneven station distribution together make spatial interpolation a secondary concern. Any spatial disaggregation of the city-median forecast is deferred to future work contingent on a spatially balanced network expansion and reference co-location data.

### 1.4.6. Method selection rationale

The preceding five subsections establish the set of available methods. The selection of XGBoost as the primary model in the present study is determined by the intersection of five constraints specific to the Almaty problem.

The first constraint is dataset size. The training set contains approximately 9,200 hourly rows spanning October 2024 to April 2026, a moderately sized tabular dataset that is too large for reliable ARIMA fitting with many covariates (parameter estimates become poorly conditioned above roughly 1,000–2,000 rows for models with 20+ parameters) but too small to train deep LSTM or transformer models without high risk of overfitting. Published empirical comparisons of GBDT versus LSTM on datasets in the 5,000–15,000 row range consistently favour GBDT, often by margins of 10–20% in RMSE \cite{Ayus2023Comparison}. XGBoost sits at the centre of the dataset size range where its regularisation most effectively exploits the available training signal.

The second constraint is feature heterogeneity. The model feature matrix combines continuous meteorological variables (temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, BLH from the Open-Meteo forecast API \cite{zippenfenig_openmeteo_2023}), cyclical trigonometric encodings of hour-of-day and day-of-year, autoregressive PM2.5 lags, and a binary heating-season indicator. Gradient-boosted trees handle such heterogeneous, mixed-type feature matrices natively, the axis-aligned splits in decision trees apply equally to continuous, ordinal, and binary features without normalisation, embedding, or one-hot encoding overhead. Neural networks require either separate embedding layers for categorical features or normalisation of all continuous inputs to comparable scales, adding engineering complexity that is not justified by the scale of this problem.

The third constraint is physical interpretability. The research question RQ2 asks whether BLH ranks among the top predictors, a question that requires a well-defined and computationally efficient feature importance measure. XGBoost's built-in gain-based importance and its compatibility with the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) framework \cite{lundberg2017shap} provide exactly this. SHAP values for XGBoost are computed in O(TLD) time, where T is the number of trees, L is the maximum leaf count, and D is the depth, making the feature attribution analysis computationally negligible relative to training time.

The fourth constraint is deployment target. The forecast system is implemented as a Streamlit web application hosted on the Streamlit Community Cloud free tier, which provides a single shared CPU and 1 GB of RAM. The three trained XGBoost models (one per horizon: 6, 12, and 24 hours) serialise to approximately 2 MB each and produce a forecast within 20 milliseconds at inference time. LSTM and transformer models at comparable capacity would require either CPU-optimised export or substantially reduced layer counts that might negate their accuracy advantage.

The fifth constraint is the non-linearity of the target process. The BLH-driven concentration dynamics described in §1.1.1 involve threshold effects and multiplicative interactions that linear models cannot represent. Decision tree ensembles capture these non-linearities through hierarchical axis-aligned splits: the tree for the 6-hour model can learn, for example, that when BLH < 200 m AND hour-of-day is between 06:00 and 09:00 AND heating-season = 1, the expected PM2.5 increment over six hours is substantially larger than the unconditional mean. This conditional interaction is precisely the inversion-onset signature that motivates the forecast system.

Taken together, these five constraints form a coherent argument for XGBoost that is specific to the data-availability profile and deployment context of the present work. The argument is not that XGBoost is the universally optimal method for PM2.5 forecasting, deep learning approaches may well outperform it in settings with longer histories, denser reference networks, and GPU-capable deployment infrastructure. The argument is that XGBoost is the optimal method for this specific problem.

## 1.5. Problem statement

### 1.5.1. Synthesis of the gap

The four preceding sections establish four converging facts that define the research gap addressed by the present work.

Almaty has a severe, seasonally concentrated PM2.5 problem driven by well-characterised physical mechanisms: a mountain-basin geometry that traps cold-pool inversions, a residential coal-heating sector that dominates wintertime fine-particle emissions, and a vehicle fleet that compounds the diurnal concentration structure (§1.1). The city's 2 million residents are exposed to PM2.5 concentrations that exceed the WHO 24-hour guideline \cite{who_aqg_2021} of 15 μg/m³ on the majority of days from October through March. Monthly mean concentrations of 80.7 μg/m³ (December 2025) and 74.2 μg/m³ (January 2026) represent exceedances of this guideline by factors of five to six. Short-term forecasting could enable actionable population-protection measures, school closures, traffic restriction advisories, voluntary heating reduction, but only if the forecast is available with sufficient lead time, accuracy, and credibility to inform decisions.

The only open, hourly, city-scale data source for Almaty is the OpenAQ LCS network of 192 stations \cite{openaq_platform}, which carries documented quality limitations (humidity bias, inter-unit variability, potential drift) but is the sole viable input for a reproducible, real-time forecasting pipeline. Kazhydromet's reference network is too sparse and not openly accessible in near-real time. Commercial platforms operate without published methodology.

The appropriate modelling tool for a 9,200-row tabular dataset with mixed feature types and a non-linear meteorological target process is gradient-boosted tree regression, specifically XGBoost, for the reasons established in §1.4.6. Deep learning approaches are not well-suited to this dataset size; physics-based CTMs cannot resolve the sub-grid inversion dynamics that drive concentration variability; statistical ARIMA models cannot represent the non-linear BLH-concentration interaction.

Despite this convergence of need, data availability, and method suitability, no published, open, and reproducible short-term PM2.5 forecasting system exists for Almaty. This is the gap the present work fills.

### 1.5.2. Research questions

Three research questions follow from the gap statement and from the methodological discussion in §1.4.

**RQ1.** Can an XGBoost model trained on OpenAQ LCS observations and Open-Meteo meteorological features, including boundary-layer height, outperform both a persistence baseline and the CAMS global reanalysis forecast for the 6, 12, and 24-hour forecast horizons in Almaty, as measured by RMSE, MAE, and coefficient of determination R²?

This question establishes whether the data-driven approach is viable at all. If the model cannot beat persistence, the trivial benchmark, then the LCS observations, Open-Meteo features, or the forecasting horizon are not informative enough to justify the modelling investment. If it cannot beat CAMS, then the global physics-based system already captures the Almaty dynamics well enough that local data-driven modelling adds no value. The result in §3.2, a 41% RMSE reduction relative to CAMS at the 6-hour horizon, establishes that both benchmarks are substantially exceeded.

**RQ2.** Which meteorological and autoregressive features drive model skill? Specifically, does boundary-layer height rank among the top predictors, consistent with the physical argument (§1.1.1) that inversion depth is the principal control on Almaty's wintertime PM2.5 regime?

This question connects the empirical model outputs back to the physical understanding of the problem. A model that achieves low RMSE by memorising the seasonal cycle (heating-season indicator) without capturing the within-season inversion dynamics would be less scientifically valuable and less operationally useful, it would fail to anticipate sudden inversion-onset events. If BLH ranks highly in SHAP-based feature importance, this constitutes quantitative support for the physical mechanism proposed in §1.1.1 and provides confidence that the model extrapolates sensibly to new inversion events.

**RQ3.** What is the residual error distribution under the best-performing model, and in which physical regimes, inversion onset, sustained episode, rapid clearing, does the model fail systematically?

This question is diagnostic. Even a model that achieves good aggregate RMSE may have structured, regime-dependent errors that are important for understanding its operational limitations. Systematic under-prediction during inversion onset, for example, would imply that the model can track an established episode but cannot anticipate its initiation, a limitation with direct implications for early-warning applications where 12–24 hour lead time is needed before concentrations cross the 75 μg/m³ emergency threshold.

### 1.5.3. Hypotheses

Three testable hypotheses follow from the physical setting and the literature review.

**H1.** XGBoost outperforms the persistence baseline on all three forecast horizons in both RMSE and MAE. At the 12-hour horizon, persistence is expected to degrade particularly sharply relative to the shorter horizons because the strong positive autocorrelation at 1–3 hours weakens substantially by 12 hours as boundary-layer growth and afternoon mixing decorrelate the morning and afternoon concentration values. The XGBoost model, which can condition on forecast-time BLH and hour-of-day to anticipate this afternoon mixing effect, should therefore gain its largest advantage over persistence in the 12-hour horizon window.

**H2.** BLH and BLH-derived features, the log-transform of BLH, which stabilises the extreme right-skew of the training-window distribution (range 10–3,935 m, mean 257 m), and the hour-over-hour BLH delta, appear among the top five SHAP feature importances at the 6-hour forecast horizon. This hypothesis is grounded in the physical argument of §1.1.1 that the boundary-layer depth is the primary variable controlling the dilution volume available for surface-layer pollutants during winter inversion episodes. If this hypothesis is rejected, if BLH ranks below the autoregressive lags in importance, it would suggest that the model learns primarily from recent PM2.5 history rather than from forward-looking meteorological state, and the BLH feature should be retained on physical grounds despite its low statistical importance.

**H3.** The XGBoost model systematically under-predicts PM2.5 during the initial 2–6 hours of inversion onset events, defined as periods when BLH falls below 100 m following a period of at least 6 hours above 300 m. The physical reasoning is that during onset, the autoregressive lag features reflect pre-inversion concentrations that are substantially lower than the concentrations accumulating in the collapsing surface layer. The BLH feature signals the onset, but the model's gradient-boosted tree structure cannot fully extrapolate the concentration ramp beyond the maximum ramp rates observed in the training set. This structured error regime is the primary expected operational limitation of the system and motivates the residual analysis in §3.4.

### 1.5.4. Scope boundaries

The present work is bounded by four explicit scope limits that are stated here to prevent misinterpretation of the results.

First, the forecast target is the cross-sensor median PM2.5 across the 192-station OpenAQ network, not a reference-grade mass concentration traceable to gravimetric standards. All reported error metrics are relative to this LCS-derived target. They cannot be directly compared to RMSE figures from studies that use regulatory reference instruments as validation targets without accounting for the additional uncertainty introduced by the LCS target bias (estimated at ±20–50% during high-RH conditions, §1.3.2).

Second, the model is trained and evaluated exclusively on the Almaty domain for the period 2024-10-01 to 2026-04-15. No claim of geographic generalisability is made. Transfer to other Central Asian cities, even those with broadly similar inversion-dominated meteorology, would require retraining on local data. The 562-day training window covers two full heating seasons (October–March 2024–25 and October 2025 through April 2026) and one full summer-to-winter transition, which provides meaningful coverage of the dominant seasonal modes. It does not, however, cover long-term inter-annual variability: the 2024–25 winter may differ from a 10-year climatological winter in the frequency and severity of inversion events. Model skill reported in §3.2 should therefore be interpreted as in-sample-climate performance, not as a guarantee of skill under climatically anomalous future winters.

Third, the study does not attempt spatial forecasting, predicting concentration at specific locations within the city as a function of local source proximity and wind direction. The city-median forecast is appropriate for population-level exposure guidance but not for neighbourhood-scale hotspot identification.

Fourth, the Streamlit deployment described in Chapter 3 is a research prototype, not a production system. It has not undergone the security review, load testing, or uptime monitoring required of a public health advisory system. Chapter 4 discusses the additional steps needed to move from research prototype to operational service.

## 1.6. Almaty in the context of global urban air quality research

The analytical review presented in §§1.1–1.5 situates Almaty within a global body of research that has developed primarily around North American, European, and East Asian cities. Understanding how Almaty's case relates to and diverges from this mainstream literature is necessary to contextualise the methodological choices made in the present work and to identify where its results are generalisable versus Almaty-specific.

### 1.6.1. Comparison with other inversion-dominated cities

Several cities worldwide share Almaty's principal air quality characteristics: a mountain-basin topography that promotes cold-air pooling, a coal-heavy residential heating sector, and a winter PM2.5 regime driven by thermally forced boundary-layer collapse. The most extensively studied examples are Salt Lake City (Utah, USA), Santiago (Chile), and several cities in the Po Valley of northern Italy. Salt Lake City has been the site of numerous inversion-specific forecasting studies that confirm the primacy of BLH as a predictor variable \cite{Silcox2012winterPM25, Whiteman2014SLV}. Santiago has been modelled with both WRF-Chem and statistical downscaling approaches, where the correlation between afternoon mixing-layer depth and next-day PM2.5 exceeds 0.7 in winter \cite{menares2021forecasting}. The Po Valley inversion studies, which benefit from the dense operational monitoring of the Lombardy ARPA network, provide the most rigorous tests of BLH-based gradient-boosting models and report 6-hour R² values of 0.55–0.70 \cite{stafoggia2019estimation}.

Two features distinguish Almaty from these comparators. First, the data infrastructure is substantially weaker: Salt Lake City has reference-grade tapered-element oscillating microbalance (TEOM) instruments at 12 monitoring sites within the valley; Almaty's reference network contains fewer than 10 stations, none of which exposes an open real-time API. Second, the heating-season emission structure is more sharply concentrated in private-sector solid-fuel combustion in Almaty than in the North American or European comparators, where natural gas heating and district heating networks dominate. This concentration makes the heating-season binary indicator a stronger predictor in Almaty than it would be in a European comparator city, and it means that emission inventory uncertainty is higher because individual household stove usage is not metered.

### 1.6.2. Related work in Central Asia and the former Soviet Union

Air quality research in Central Asian cities has accelerated since 2020 but remains sparse compared to East Asian or European urban studies. Published machine-learning forecasting studies are absent for Almaty and its immediate regional comparators. The available literature falls into three categories.

Physical and chemical characterisation studies have analysed the aerosol composition of Almaty's pollution episodes, identifying black carbon, mineral dust, and secondary inorganic aerosols as the dominant mass fractions \cite{Kerimray2020, Tursumbayeva2022}. These studies confirm the coal-combustion signature in the carbonaceous fraction during winter and the dust signature in spring, providing supporting evidence for the seasonally stratified feature design in the present work.

Remote-sensing based assessments using MODIS aerosol optical depth (AOD) retrievals have documented the spatial footprint of Almaty's pollution plume at 10 km resolution \cite{Kerimray2020}. These studies establish that the pollution plume extends north-east from the city into the steppe under dominant south-westerly flow, but the 10 km retrieval footprint is insufficient for the intra-urban spatial analysis that would be needed to validate a neighbourhood-scale forecast. The ground-level PM2.5–AOD conversion also carries substantial uncertainty in the presence of temperature inversions, because the satellite-measured column AOD integrates over all vertical levels and cannot distinguish a shallow surface-layer concentration spike from a deep but dilute aerosol layer.

Ulaanbaatar (Mongolia) is the closest published comparator to Almaty: a former Soviet city at similar latitude, similar topographic setting, similarly coal-dependent residential heating, and a growing LCS network on OpenAQ. Published gradient-boosting studies on Ulaanbaatar data report 6-hour MAE values of 18–25 μg/m³ for winter test periods \cite{enebish2021ulaanbaatar}, consistent with the MAE of 16.81 μg/m³ achieved by XGBoost in the present study, given the different sensor networks and training window durations. This convergence suggests that the performance levels reported here are not anomalously optimistic: they are consistent with what gradient-boosting models achieve in analogous physically and data-wise constrained environments.

### 1.6.3. Gap summary and contribution positioning

The reviewed literature reveals three specific gaps that the present work addresses. First, no published forecasting study has used Almaty's OpenAQ LCS network as the training and evaluation data source, with an explicit comparison against CAMS as the freely available global alternative. Second, no Central Asian city study has systematically evaluated the trade-off between linear and non-linear models as a function of forecast horizon in an LCS-based, moderate-training-set regime. Third, no open-source pipeline for city-scale PM2.5 forecasting in the OpenAQ data ecosystem has been published for any Central Asian city. The present work addresses all three gaps simultaneously through a single reproducible codebase, making its contributions identifiable and testable by future researchers in the region.

Chapter 2 specifies the data sources, ingest pipeline, feature engineering decisions, and model training procedure through which the three hypotheses stated above are tested and the four scope boundaries are operationalised.
