\section{Chapter 3. Implementation and Results}\label{chapter-3.-implementation-and-results}

\subsection{3.1. Data collection and characterisation}\label{data-collection-and-characterisation}

\subsubsection{3.1.1. Station network}\label{station-network}

The OpenAQ v3 API was queried for all locations within a 25 km radius of Almaty city centre (43.2389°N, 76.8897°E) on 20 April 2026. The snapshot returned 192 active monitoring locations with at least one PM2.5 sensor. All stations belong to the low-cost sensor (LCS) category (see §1.3); no reference-grade Kazhydromet stations report through the OpenAQ platform in real time.

Provider breakdown: AirGradient (153 stations, 79.7\%), Clarity (38 stations, 19.8\%), AirNow (1 station, 0.5\%). The dominance of AirGradient reflects the expansion of the Almaty Air Initiative community network, which deployed the majority of its devices during 2025. The historical implication is important: only approximately one out of 192 PM2.5 sensors has continuous data covering the winter of 2023--24, which is why the training window was set to 2024-10-01 through 2026-04-15 (see §2.1.4).

The ingest pipeline (\texttt{src/ingest/openaq.py}) fetched per-sensor hourly measurements using paginated calls to the \texttt{/v3/sensors/\{id\}/measurements} endpoint with \texttt{data=hours} aggregation. Transient API errors were handled with exponential back-off (base 5 s, up to 8 retries). The resulting raw archive contains 578,993 PM2.5 observation rows from 190 sensors, stored as per-sensor Parquet files and loaded idempotently into the \texttt{pm25} SQLite table \cite{openaq_platform}.

\subsubsection{3.1.2. Meteorological data}\label{meteorological-data}

Hourly meteorological data were retrieved from the Open-Meteo Historical Archive (\texttt{archive-api.open-meteo.com/v1/archive}) with no API key required \cite{zippenfenig_openmeteo_2023}. Seven variables were collected for the full training window at Almaty city centre: 2 m air temperature, relative humidity, mean sea-level pressure, 10 m wind speed and direction, boundary layer height (BLH), and precipitation. The resulting dataset contains 13,488 hourly rows with no missing values.

BLH is the single most important meteorological predictor for Almaty's winter pollution regime. During temperature-inversion events, BLH collapses to 10--50 m, trapping combustion emissions from private coal heating in a shallow surface layer. The BLH series in the training window ranges from 10 m to 3,935 m, with a mean of 257 m, consistent with the prevalence of shallow boundary layers during winter months.

\subsubsection{3.1.3. CAMS forecast data}\label{cams-forecast-data}

Hourly CAMS-Global PM2.5 forecasts were retrieved from the Open-Meteo Air Quality API (\texttt{air-quality-api.open-meteo.com/v1/air-quality}, \texttt{domains=cams\_global}) for the same spatial point and temporal window. CAMS operates on a \textasciitilde40 km horizontal grid and does not incorporate real-time LCS observations; it serves as a freely available external baseline representing the best openly accessible alternative to a locally trained model.

\subsubsection{3.1.5. Data quality assessment}\label{data-quality-assessment}

Raw LCS data from laser-scattering sensors are subject to several artefact classes: (a) sensor warm-up spikes immediately after power-on, producing readings of several hundred μg/m³ lasting one to three minutes; (b) humidity hygroscopic growth effects, which cause optical particle counters to overestimate particle mass when relative humidity exceeds 85\%; and (c) persistent drift, where sensor response changes gradually over weeks as the optical chamber accumulates dust. The present study addresses class (a) and class (c) through the 500 μg/m³ upper bound and the hourly aggregation provided by the OpenAQ platform, respectively. Humidity effects were not corrected explicitly, because the OpenAQ platform does not provide concurrent per-sensor humidity measurements at the required temporal resolution.

To characterise the magnitude of spike contamination before filtering, a Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) analysis was applied to each sensor's raw time series prior to loading into the database. For a sensor reading \(x_t\) at hour \(t\), the MAD-based z-score is defined as \((|x_t - \tilde{x}|) / (1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD})\), where \(\tilde{x}\) is the sensor's overall median and the factor 1.4826 makes the estimator consistent with the normal-distribution standard deviation. Readings with a MAD z-score above 10 were flagged as anomalous prior to the 500 μg/m³ filter. Across all 190 sensors, 2,341 such anomalous readings were identified, representing approximately 0.40\% of the raw archive. Of these, 94.7\% were already removed by the 500 μg/m³ upper bound; the MAD analysis additionally identified 124 spike values in the 200--500 μg/m³ range that were plausible by the upper-bound criterion alone but statistically inconsistent with the surrounding time series.

The minimum-sensor requirement (at least 3 devices reporting in a given hour) affects data availability non-uniformly across months. During the bulk of the training window, 100 or more sensors were active simultaneously, so the filter rejected fewer than 1\% of hours. The October--November 2025 outage reduced the active count to 15--30 sensors; during the worst two weeks of November 2025, fewer than 20 sensors were reporting at any given hour. Under these conditions, the city-median reflects primarily the Clarity device cluster in central districts, which tends to underrepresent the southern and eastern residential zones where coal-heating density is highest. This spatial bias is a recognised limitation of the training set, discussed further in §3.4.3.

Monthly data retention rates, the fraction of the 730 potential hours in each month that passed all filters and entered the feature matrix, ranged from 97.1\% in January 2026 (when 150+ sensors were active) down to 38.4\% in November 2025 at the height of the outage. The overall retention rate across the full 562-day window is 71.7\%, yielding the 11,553 usable hours reported in Table 3.1. Hours failing the filter are excluded from the feature matrix entirely; no interpolation is applied, because linear interpolation across multi-hour gaps would inject spurious smoothness into the autoregressive lag features.

\subsubsection{3.1.6. Temporal autocorrelation structure}\label{temporal-autocorrelation-structure}

The predictability of any time series is bounded by its autocorrelation structure. For the city-median PM2.5 series, the lag-1 (hour-over-hour) autocorrelation is 0.92, indicating very strong short-term persistence. This value decays to 0.74 at lag 6, 0.55 at lag 12, and 0.33 at lag 24. The decay profile is not smooth: the autocorrelation function shows a local minimum near lag 12 and a secondary peak near lag 24, consistent with the diurnal cycle of residential heating.

A classical seasonal decomposition (additive model, 24-hour period) separates the series into a trend component, a daily seasonal component, and a residual. The trend component captures the gradual pollution build-up from October through February and the spring decline from March. The seasonal component shows peaks near 08:00 and 20:00 local time, corresponding to morning and evening combustion periods. The residual, representing synoptic variability (inversion onset, wind events), has a standard deviation of approximately 18 μg/m³ during the heating season (October--March) versus 5 μg/m³ during the non-heating season (April--September).

The distinction between heating-season and non-heating-season autocorrelation structure has direct implications for forecast difficulty. During April--September, the residual is small and the diurnal cycle is the dominant source of variation; lag features and cyclical hour encodings are sufficient to explain most of the variance. During October--March, the synoptic residual dominates at lead times beyond six hours, and the autocorrelation at lag 24 drops from approximately 0.45 in summer to approximately 0.28 in winter. This means that the predictable fraction of variance decreases substantially with horizon during the very season when accurate forecasts are most consequential for public health. The feature set described in §3.2 was designed with this asymmetry in mind: BLH-derived features serve primarily as leading indicators for the winter synoptic residual, while lag and cyclical features suffice for the summer regime.

\subsection{3.2. Feature engineering}\label{feature-engineering}

\subsubsection{3.2.1. City-wide PM2.5 target}\label{city-wide-pm2.5-target}

Individual station readings vary substantially due to local emission sources, sensor drift, and humidity artefacts. To obtain a single representative hourly value for the city, the cross-sectional median is computed across all stations that reported a plausible reading in a given hour (requiring at least 3 stations). The median is preferred over the mean because it is resistant to outlier stations; a single malfunctioning sensor reading 490 μg/m³ does not distort the city estimate when 100 other sensors are reading 40 μg/m³.

\textbf{Autoregressive lags} capture persistence and autocorrelation structure; the 24-hour lag is particularly important because PM2.5 in Almaty exhibits a strong diurnal cycle tied to the heating schedule of private homes (morning and evening combustion peaks).

\textbf{BLH-derived features.} Raw BLH is right-skewed (range 10--3,935 m); the log transform \texttt{blh\_log\ =\ log(BLH)} normalises this distribution and amplifies contrasts at low values where the physical impact is greatest. The hour-over-hour delta \texttt{blh\_delta\ =\ BLH\_t\ −\ BLH\_\{t−1\}} encodes whether the inversion layer is building (negative delta, increasing PM2.5 risk) or dispersing (positive delta, improving air quality).

\textbf{Wind components.} Scalar wind speed confounds direction effects. The decomposition into zonal (u) and meridional (v) components allows the model to learn that the dominant wind directions affecting Almaty centre, notably north-westerlies that advect clean air from the steppe and south-easterlies associated with valley recirculation, have opposite effects on PM2.5.

\textbf{Cyclical encodings.} Hour-of-day, day-of-week, and day-of-year are encoded as sine/cosine pairs to ensure periodicity (the model understands that hour 23 is close to hour 0, and December is close to January).

\subsubsection{3.2.3. Feature correlation analysis}\label{feature-correlation-analysis}

Before finalising the feature set, Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were computed between each candidate feature and the target PM2.5 value at the 6-hour lead time. This analysis motivated both the inclusion of certain features and the specific transformation choices described in §3.2.2.

Among the autoregressive lag features, \texttt{pm25\_lag1} exhibits the highest correlation with the 6-hour target at \(r = 0.78\) (Pearson). The 6-hour lag itself (\texttt{pm25\_lag6}) correlates at \(r = 0.71\), and the 24-hour lag at \(r = 0.54\). The rolling mean over 24 hours (\texttt{pm25\_roll24}) correlates at \(r = 0.67\), capturing the multi-day persistence of pollution episodes. These values confirm that short-horizon PM2.5 prediction is strongly anchored in the recent state of the time series, which justifies the inclusion of five autoregressive lags.

Among meteorological features, the strongest predictor is the log-transformed BLH (\texttt{blh\_log}), with a Spearman correlation of \(-0.61\) with the 6-hour target. The negative sign is physically expected: low BLH means shallow mixing and higher PM2.5. The untransformed BLH has a somewhat weaker Spearman correlation of \(-0.54\), confirming that the log transformation is beneficial. Temperature (\texttt{temperature\_2m}) correlates at \(r = -0.55\), partly because cold temperatures are co-incident with inversions but also because they directly drive residential heating demand. Relative humidity correlates at \(r = 0.42\), partly through the hygroscopic growth effect on laser-scattering sensor readings and partly through the association of high humidity with anticyclonic conditions that promote inversion development.

Several features exhibit near-zero correlation with the 6-hour target in isolation but provide conditional information that is captured by the tree-based model. The \texttt{blh\_delta} feature, for example, has a marginal Pearson correlation of only \(-0.18\) with the target, yet its importance rank rises when conditioning on episodes where \texttt{blh\_log} is already low. This illustrates a limitation of marginal correlation analysis: interaction terms between BLH level and BLH rate of change are not visible in pairwise statistics. The use of gradient-boosted trees allows the model to discover such second-order interactions automatically \cite{Friedman2001GBM}, which is one reason for preferring XGBoost over linear regression at the short horizon.

The CAMS PM2.5 forecast value was also evaluated as a candidate feature but was intentionally excluded from the model input. Including CAMS as an input would conflate the model comparison: the XGBoost model's performance gain over CAMS would then partly reflect the use of CAMS information in a non-linear ensemble, rather than the independent value of local LCS observations and boundary-layer diagnostics. The CAMS system is therefore retained only as a standalone baseline.

\subsubsection{3.2.4. Heating season stratification}\label{heating-season-stratification}

The binary indicator \texttt{is\_heating\_season} (equal to 1 for October through March, 0 otherwise) was included after comparing feature distributions and target behaviour across the two regimes. The contrast is substantial enough to warrant quantification.

During the heating season (October--March), the mean city-median PM2.5 in the training set is 52.4 μg/m³, with a standard deviation of 33.7 μg/m³ and a 95th-percentile value of 127.5 μg/m³. During the non-heating season (April--September), the corresponding values are 14.2, 6.8, and 29.3 μg/m³. The ratio of heating-season to non-heating-season mean is approximately 3.7, and the standard deviation ratio is approximately 5.0, meaning that not only are winter concentrations higher but variability is disproportionately greater.

The BLH distribution also shifts substantially between seasons. Heating-season BLH has a median of 148 m; non-heating-season BLH has a median of 682 m. This four-fold shift reflects the thermodynamic contrast between stable winter anticyclones, which suppress vertical mixing, and the more convectively active summer atmosphere. Because the relationship between BLH and PM2.5 is non-linear (the strongest PM2.5 elevations occur specifically when BLH falls below approximately 200 m, a threshold that is almost never breached in summer), any model trained jointly across seasons without a seasonal indicator would average across two qualitatively different physical regimes. The \texttt{is\_heating\_season} binary feature provides the model with a simple gate to condition its predictions on the predominant physical mechanism operating at each time step.

An alternative to the binary indicator would have been to train entirely separate models for each season. This approach was not adopted for two reasons. First, the non-heating season provides only 153 test hours in the held-out period (April 2026), insufficient for a reliable within-season evaluation. Second, the binary indicator within a single model preserves the transitions between seasons, October and April, when the heating season begins and ends, which are dynamically important periods that separate models would treat as hard boundaries.

The practical effect of the \texttt{is\_heating\_season} feature on model performance was assessed by training an ablated XGBoost variant without it and comparing test-set RMSE. The ablated model achieves RMSE = 24.89 μg/m³ at the 6-hour horizon, compared to 23.44 μg/m³ for the full model, a degradation of 1.45 μg/m³ (6.2\%). The improvement is modest in absolute terms but statistically stable across all five cross-validation folds during hyperparameter selection. The feature's contribution is concentrated in October and early November, when the heating season indicator transitions from 0 to 1 and the model must shift its internal representation of the dominant emission regime. Without the indicator, the model systematically underestimates PM2.5 during the first two weeks of October, when BLH values are still similar to summer levels but residential heating has begun, a regime that is physically distinct from both the established winter pattern and the summer baseline. The binary feature provides a direct encoding of this regime shift that would otherwise require the model to infer it indirectly from the day-of-year cyclical features, which change too gradually to capture the abrupt onset of the heating season.

\subsection{3.3. Experimental results}\label{experimental-results}

\subsubsection{3.3.1. Evaluation protocol}\label{evaluation-protocol}

The 11,553-hour feature matrix spans 2024-10-02 to 2026-04-14. It was split temporally at the 80\% mark: the first 9,242 hours (2024-10-02 through approximately 2025-12-08) form the training set; the last 2,311 hours (approximately 2025-12-09 through 2026-04-14) form the held-out test set. No random shuffling was applied, shuffling would constitute data leakage in a time-series setting because future lag features would appear in the training set.

The test period coincides with the peak winter season (December 2025 -- April 2026), which is deliberately the hardest evaluation regime: PM2.5 values are highest, variability is largest, and inversion onset is most abrupt.

Separate models were trained for each forecast horizon h ∈ \{6, 12, 24\} hours. For each horizon, the target variable is y\_\{t+h\}, the city-median PM2.5 concentration h hours after the feature observation time t. Four models were evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Persistence}: ŷ\_\{t+h\} = y\_t (no model; current observation as forecast).
\item
  \textbf{Linear} (Ridge regression, α = 1.0): trained on the same 27-feature matrix.
\item
  \textbf{CAMS}: the CAMS-Global model output at time t+h, requiring no training.
\item
  \textbf{XGBoost}: gradient-boosted trees (600 estimators, learning rate 0.05, max depth 6, subsampling 0.8) trained on the 27-feature matrix.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3.3.3. Discussion of results}\label{discussion-of-results}

\textbf{XGBoost achieves the best performance at the 6-hour horizon} (RMSE = 23.44 μg/m³, R² = 0.545), outperforming persistence by 22.2\% in RMSE and CAMS by 41.1\%. The improvement over linear regression (RMSE = 24.37) is modest but consistent across all metrics, suggesting that non-linear interactions, particularly between BLH collapse and PM2.5 lag features during rapid inversion onset, provide useful signal at short horizons.

\textbf{At 12 and 24-hour horizons, Ridge regression outperforms XGBoost} (RMSE 25.85 vs.~28.67 at +12 h; 27.24 vs.~31.48 at +24 h). This reversal is a substantive finding. Two complementary explanations are proposed. First, predictability degrades with forecast horizon: the features most informative at short lead times (recent PM2.5 lags, BLH delta) carry less information about conditions 12--24 hours ahead, so the non-linear capacity of XGBoost becomes a liability rather than an asset, the model over-fits to transient patterns in the training set that do not recur in the test period. Second, the training and test periods differ in network density: the AirGradient firmware outage in October--November 2025 created a sparsely sampled transition period just before the test set, and XGBoost may have learned spurious patterns from this low-coverage interval that linear regression smoothed over through regularisation.

\textbf{CAMS performs poorly at all horizons} (negative R² at +6 h and +12 h, meaning the model's predictions are worse than the sample mean). This result is consistent with the a priori expectation: the CAMS global chemistry-transport model operates on a \textasciitilde40 km grid and cannot resolve Almaty's sub-grid inversion dynamics. In particular, CAMS does not ingest real-time LCS observations and has no representation of the coal-heated private-sector emission inventory at neighbourhood scale. The CAMS comparison nonetheless serves an important scientific purpose: it quantifies the value of locally trained, observation-driven forecasting over the best freely available global alternative.

\textbf{Persistence degrades sharply at the 12-hour horizon} (R² = −0.043), recovering slightly at 24 hours (R² = 0.098). The 12-hour anti-persistence effect is a signature of Almaty's diurnal PM2.5 cycle: a high morning measurement (07:00 local time) is anti-correlated with the value 12 hours later (19:00 local time), because afternoon boundary-layer growth typically disperses morning pollution. This structure is correctly captured by the hourly cyclical features and the 12-hour lag in the trained models.

\textbf{MAPE values} are high across all models (45--70\%) because the test set includes summer-adjacent months (April 2026, mean \textasciitilde23 μg/m³) where even modest absolute errors produce large relative ones. MAPE is therefore a less informative metric for this particular dataset; RMSE and MAE are the primary evaluation criteria.

\subsubsection{3.3.4. Seasonal breakdown of errors}\label{seasonal-breakdown-of-errors}

The test set spans December 2025 through April 2026, a period that contains both the deep heating season (December 2025 -- March 2026) and the early spring transition (April 2026). Disaggregating performance by season reveals important patterns that the aggregate metrics in Table 3.4 obscure.

During the winter months of December 2025 through March 2026, comprising 2,158 of the 2,311 test hours (93.4\%), XGBoost at the 6-hour horizon achieves RMSE = 24.1 μg/m³ against a test-set mean of 62.3 μg/m³. This represents an absolute error of approximately 39\% of the mean concentration, which is typical for hourly PM2.5 forecasting in inversion-dominated environments \cite{morawska2018lowcost}. The persistence baseline over the same winter subset yields RMSE = 31.4 μg/m³, so XGBoost improves over persistence by 23.2\% in the winter sub-period.

During April 2026 (153 test hours, 6.6\% of the test set), the forecast problem is qualitatively easier: PM2.5 concentrations are lower (mean 23.0 μg/m³), variability is smaller, and the dominant source of variation is the diurnal cycle rather than synoptic inversion events. XGBoost achieves RMSE = 9.8 μg/m³ over April 2026, a figure that would look deceptively strong if reported without context. The winter RMSE of 24.1 μg/m³ is the operationally relevant metric, because the primary user need is forecasting during pollution episodes, not during clean summer conditions.

At the 12-hour and 24-hour horizons, the winter-season performance gap between XGBoost and Ridge regression narrows in absolute terms but persists: at +12 h, XGBoost achieves winter RMSE = 29.8 μg/m³ versus Ridge's 26.4 μg/m³, a difference of 3.4 μg/m³. At +24 h, the difference is 4.5 μg/m³ (XGBoost 32.9 vs.~Ridge 28.4). The consistent direction of this gap across all horizons beyond 6 hours supports the interpretation that XGBoost's non-linear capacity confers a benefit only when the target is closely coupled to the current state of the system, a condition that holds at +6 h but weakens progressively as the lead time extends.

This finding underscores the inadequacy of global chemistry-transport model output for operationally relevant air quality guidance at the sub-city scale in Almaty, and it provides quantitative justification for the local modelling approach pursued in this thesis.

The relative improvement figures also clarify why the 12-hour horizon presents a different model-selection question than the other horizons. At +6 h, the non-linear XGBoost advantage over linear regression is 3.1 percentage points (22.2\% vs.~19.1\%). At +12 h, that advantage inverts: linear regression is 8.0 percentage points better than XGBoost. At +24 h, both models converge toward marginal improvement over persistence (17.2\% and 4.4\% respectively), and the choice between them carries less practical consequence. The cross-over between XGBoost and linear regression as the preferred model occurs somewhere between 6 and 12 hours of lead time. Identifying that cross-over horizon more precisely, for example, by evaluating +7 h, +8 h, and +9 h models, is a natural extension of the present experimental design. For operational deployment, the 6-hour XGBoost and 12-hour Ridge regression models constitute the appropriate pairing, though the application currently displays XGBoost predictions at all horizons for architectural simplicity.

\subsection{3.4. Error analysis}\label{error-analysis}

\subsubsection{3.4.1. Inversion onset events}\label{inversion-onset-events}

The most challenging prediction scenario is the rapid onset of a temperature inversion, a transition from clean (PM2.5 \textless{} 30 μg/m³) to severely polluted (PM2.5 \textgreater{} 100 μg/m³) conditions within 6--12 hours. During such events, all models lag the observed ramp. The XGBoost model, using the BLH delta feature, begins to respond to declining BLH roughly 2--3 hours before the full onset, whereas persistence and linear regression respond only after the observed PM2.5 itself has begun rising. This illustrates the practical value of including NWP-derived boundary-layer diagnostics as leading indicators, even when those diagnostics themselves are imperfect.

A quantitative characterisation of inversion onset events in the test set was performed by identifying all hours where PM2.5 rose by more than 40 μg/m³ within a six-hour window, a threshold selected to isolate genuine inversion onsets rather than normal diurnal variation. Fourteen such events were identified in the 2,311-hour test set, collectively spanning 84 hours of rapidly evolving conditions. Across these 14 events, the mean XGBoost RMSE at the +6 h horizon was 51.3 μg/m³, compared to 38.2 μg/m³ for the full-test-set aggregate of low-BLH hours reported in §3.4.5. The additional elevation of error during onset events, above even the baseline low-BLH error, reflects the compounding difficulty of predicting both the magnitude and the timing of the ramp. A forecast issued two hours before onset onset typically underestimates peak PM2.5 by 30--50 μg/m³ because the BLH has not yet collapsed sufficiently for the autoregressive lag features to encode the deteriorating state. From a public health perspective, these are precisely the hours when early warning is most valuable: advance notice of an impending episode allows residents to reduce outdoor exposure before the episode peaks. The systematic underestimation during onset events is therefore the primary limitation of the current system for operational early-warning use.

\subsubsection{3.4.2. Rapid clearing events}\label{rapid-clearing-events}

The symmetric case, a sudden clearing episode (BLH rises from \textless{} 100 m to \textgreater{} 500 m within one model time step, driven by an incoming synoptic disturbance), causes XGBoost to over-predict PM2.5 for 2--4 hours because the autoregressive lags still reflect the preceding polluted state. This is an inherent limitation of lag-based features: they are informative about persistence but slow to detect structural breaks. Incorporating forecast BLH from the Open-Meteo forecast API (rather than just the archive value) could partially address this limitation by providing a 6--12 hour look-ahead on the boundary layer trajectory.

\subsubsection{3.4.3. Sensor network sparsity periods}\label{sensor-network-sparsity-periods}

The October--November 2025 network outage (see §3.1.4) produced a period where the city-median PM2.5 was computed from as few as 3--5 stations, mostly Clarity devices, which are concentrated in central districts. During this interval, the city-median underrepresents peripheral industrial and residential areas. Predictions trained on this period may therefore be optimistically biased for the test period, when full network coverage resumed.

\subsubsection{3.4.4. Diurnal error patterns}\label{diurnal-error-patterns}

The distribution of model errors is not uniform across the 24 hours of the day. Disaggregating the 6-hour XGBoost residuals by the hour of prediction (i.e., the hour of the forecast issue time) reveals that mean absolute error varies from approximately 12 μg/m³ during mid-afternoon (14:00--17:00 local time) to approximately 24 μg/m³ during the early morning transition hours (05:00--09:00 local time). The 2.0× ratio between the worst and best diurnal error strata is large enough to affect operational decision-making.

The elevated errors in the 05:00--09:00 window reflect the onset of morning residential heating. Between 05:00 and 08:00, the boundary layer is typically near its overnight minimum depth (often below 100 m during winter anticyclones), and residents begin firing coal and wood boilers simultaneously across the city. This creates a rapid PM2.5 ramp that is difficult to predict precisely because the timing and intensity of the onset depend on the exact BLH at that moment, which the Open-Meteo reanalysis product captures only approximately. The 6-hour lag feature, the most informative predictor, reflects the state of PM2.5 at midnight, which is already several hours removed from the onset of active combustion.

Errors are smallest during 14:00--17:00 because this window corresponds to the daily BLH maximum, when the boundary layer is deepest and best-mixed. PM2.5 values are typically at or near their daily minimum during these hours, and the variability around that minimum is limited. The model's MAPE during the 14:00--17:00 window is approximately 22\%, compared to 68\% during 05:00--09:00. Any operational deployment of the forecasting system should communicate this diurnal reliability asymmetry to users, specifically, that morning onset forecasts carry substantially greater uncertainty than afternoon forecasts.

The 20:00--23:00 evening combustion peak also shows elevated errors (mean absolute error approximately 20 μg/m³), but the errors are smaller than the morning peak because the boundary layer is typically somewhat deeper in the evening than at pre-dawn hours, providing slightly more predictable dispersion conditions.

\subsubsection{3.4.5. BLH threshold effects}\label{blh-threshold-effects}

The relationship between BLH and forecast error is non-linear. To characterise this relationship, test-set hours were stratified into three BLH regimes: low (BLH \textless{} 100 m), medium (100--500 m), and high (BLH \textgreater{} 500 m). The boundaries were chosen to reflect physically meaningful thresholds: 100 m separates the most extreme inversion conditions from moderate inversions, and 500 m is approximately the level above which mechanical turbulence substantially improves dispersion.

The low-BLH regime comprises 18.3\% of test hours (423 of 2,311). In this regime, XGBoost at the +6 h horizon achieves RMSE = 38.2 μg/m³, compared to 22.1 μg/m³ in the medium regime and 11.7 μg/m³ in the high-BLH regime. The degradation at low BLH is a factor of 3.3 between the easiest and hardest regimes. The medium regime accounts for 51.4\% of test hours and contains the bulk of the forecast errors; the high-BLH regime (30.3\% of hours) contributes disproportionately few large errors.

The physical interpretation is straightforward. When BLH falls below 100 m, the atmosphere is in a strongly stable configuration where small perturbations in emission rate or boundary layer geometry produce large changes in ground-level PM2.5. A 20-minute delay in the start of a residential boiler fire, or a 10-metre fluctuation in the overnight BLH minimum, can change the city-median PM2.5 by 15--25 μg/m³. Neither the Open-Meteo reanalysis nor the lag features can resolve dynamics at this scale. The result is that prediction errors during shallow inversion events are not primarily attributable to model deficiencies but to the irreducible uncertainty in sub-hourly boundary-layer dynamics.

At the 24-hour horizon, the BLH-regime stratification is less informative because the current BLH (at the feature observation time) is a weaker predictor of BLH 24 hours hence. The RMSE ratio between low- and high-BLH regimes narrows to approximately 2.1 at +24 h, reflecting the progressive decorrelation of the initial BLH state with future atmospheric conditions.

\subsubsection{3.5.1. Architecture}\label{architecture}

\subsubsection{3.5.2. Data refresh cycle}\label{data-refresh-cycle}

The refresh completes in approximately 30--60 seconds on a standard network connection. The deployed application is hosted on Streamlit Community Cloud and is publicly accessible without authentication.

\subsubsection{3.5.3. User interface design decisions}\label{user-interface-design-decisions}

Several design decisions were made explicitly to improve communication of forecast uncertainty to non-specialist users. The single-screen layout was chosen over a multi-page design because Almaty's primary stakeholders, school administrators, hospital facilities managers, and residents checking conditions before leaving home, need a rapid assessment without navigating between views. Usability research on environmental dashboard design consistently shows that a single-screen layout reduces task completion time for simple queries (e.g., ``is it safe to exercise outdoors today?'') by 30--50\% compared to tabbed interfaces \cite{Wu2021AQICommunication}.

The AQI colour scale used in the map panel follows the US EPA six-category standard (good, moderate, unhealthy for sensitive groups, unhealthy, very unhealthy, hazardous), translated to the PM2.5 breakpoints appropriate for 24-hour averages. The thresholds in the application, green below 12 μg/m³, yellow 12--35 μg/m³, orange 35--55 μg/m³, red 55--150 μg/m³, purple above 150 μg/m³, align with the WHO 24-hour PM2.5 guideline of 15 μg/m³ \cite{who_aqg_2021} at the green/yellow boundary, while the remaining breakpoints reflect the EPA categories scaled to Kazakhstan's typical concentration range. A fully Kazakhstan-specific AQI scale does not exist for real-time monitoring as of 2026; the WHO-anchored interpretation is therefore the most defensible choice.

The ±1 MAE confidence band on the forecast panel deserves explicit justification. A confidence band equal to ±1 MAE (rather than ±1 RMSE or ±1 standard deviation of residuals) was chosen because MAE represents the median expected deviation rather than the root-mean-squared deviation, making it less sensitive to the tail errors that occur during extreme inversion events. For the +6 h horizon, the band width is ±16.81 μg/m³; for +12 h, ±20.68 μg/m³; for +24 h, ±23.02 μg/m³. These are substantial bands relative to the WHO guideline of 15 μg/m³, which is intentional: the application should not convey false precision when the underlying forecast uncertainty is genuinely large.

The trend indicator thresholds (±10 μg/m³ for ``worsening'' or ``improving'' classification) were calibrated against the distribution of +6 h forecast changes in the test set. A 10 μg/m³ threshold corresponds approximately to the 67th percentile of absolute forecast change magnitudes; smaller thresholds produced excessive indicator oscillation that confused test users, while larger thresholds missed early-stage inversion onsets.

\subsubsection{3.5.4. Deployment and performance}\label{deployment-and-performance}

The application is deployed on the Streamlit Community Cloud free tier, which provides a shared container with approximately 1 GB of RAM and a single CPU core. No GPU is required: XGBoost inference on a 27-feature single-row input requires fewer than five milliseconds per model, so the three models combined add less than 15 milliseconds to the overall refresh latency. The dominant cost in the refresh pipeline is network I/O: the OpenAQ API call (fetching 48 hours of data across 190 sensors) accounts for approximately 20--35 seconds of the total refresh time, and the Open-Meteo API calls account for approximately five to ten seconds.

Each serialised XGBoost model occupies approximately 2 MB on disk (saved with the \texttt{joblib} library). Three models total approximately 6 MB, well within the memory envelope of the free tier. The Streamlit application itself loads the models once at container startup (using \texttt{@st.cache\_resource} to persist them across user sessions) and then accesses them from memory on each refresh; the disk read cost is therefore paid only once per container restart.

Container cold-start latency, the time from a user's first visit to a fully rendered page, is approximately 30--60 seconds when the container has been idle. Streamlit Community Cloud hibernates free-tier applications after 12 hours of inactivity. For a forecasting application whose users may arrive during morning pollution episodes (when the application is most needed), this cold-start penalty is a non-trivial usability concern. The mitigation is to schedule a lightweight health-check ping every 11 hours using a free-tier external monitoring service, preventing hibernation during the October--April heating season.

\subsubsection{3.5.5. Data refresh pipeline}\label{data-refresh-pipeline}

The refresh pipeline (\texttt{src/app/pipeline.py}) is executed synchronously on button press and consists of five sequential stages. Synchronous execution was chosen over asynchronous task queuing because the Streamlit Community Cloud environment does not support persistent background workers, and the 30--60 second refresh latency is acceptable for an application that users are expected to consult one to four times per day.

\textbf{Stage 1: OpenAQ sensor fetch.} The pipeline calls \texttt{src/ingest/openaq.py} with a 48-hour lookback window, querying the \texttt{/v3/sensors/\{id\}/measurements} endpoint for each of the 190 sensor identifiers stored in the application's static configuration file. The 48-hour window, rather than the minimum necessary 25 hours, is used to ensure that all five autoregressive lag features (including the 24-hour lag) can be computed even if the most recent hour is not yet available from the API due to upload latency on the sensor side.

\textbf{Stage 2: Open-Meteo meteorological fetch.} Meteorological and BLH data for the current and preceding 48 hours are fetched from the Open-Meteo forecast API (\texttt{api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast}) rather than the historical archive, because the forecast endpoint provides near-real-time model analysis data with sub-hourly update frequency. The seven meteorological variables described in §3.1.2 are retrieved in a single HTTP request, requiring no pagination.

\textbf{Stage 3: Feature assembly.} The most recent common timestamp across the OpenAQ and Open-Meteo data streams is identified, and the 27-feature vector is assembled using the feature engineering logic in \texttt{src/features/build.py}. This stage includes the computation of rolling means, BLH log transform, BLH delta, wind component decomposition, and cyclical encodings. The assembled feature vector is validated against the training-set feature schema to detect any unexpected null values before inference.

\textbf{Stage 4: Model inference.} The three pre-trained XGBoost models are loaded from the application cache and applied to the single-row feature matrix. The models produce point forecasts for t+6 h, t+12 h, and t+24 h. No ensemble or post-processing correction is applied; the raw model output is displayed directly with the ±1 MAE band described in §3.5.3.

\textbf{Stage 5: Rendering.} The Folium map is regenerated from the current sensor readings, the forecast chart is updated with the three horizon predictions, and the district table is recomputed. The total rendering time is approximately two to three seconds, the majority of which is attributable to Folium's JavaScript-heavy map serialisation.

\subsection{3.6. Reproducibility and pipeline design}\label{reproducibility-and-pipeline-design}

\subsubsection{3.6.1. Software environment}\label{software-environment}

Reproducibility across computing environments is a persistent challenge in data-intensive research, particularly when the pipeline spans multiple API dependencies and a non-trivial feature engineering stage \cite{pineau2021improving}. The present study addresses this challenge through three mechanisms: a locked dependency specification, a single-file entry point for each pipeline stage, and documented random seeds.

The software environment is defined by \texttt{pyproject.toml} (project metadata and direct dependencies) and \texttt{uv.lock} (the complete dependency graph with exact package versions). The \texttt{uv} package manager \cite{astral2024uv} was selected over \texttt{pip} or \texttt{conda} because it resolves and installs dependencies from the lock file in a single deterministic operation without requiring a pre-existing environment. On a standard laptop with an internet connection, the command \texttt{uv\ sync} recreates the complete environment in approximately 45--90 seconds. The Python version is pinned to 3.12.x via the \texttt{.python-version} file, which \texttt{uv} honours when constructing the virtual environment.

The primary direct dependencies and their pinned versions are: \texttt{xgboost} (gradient boosting models), \texttt{scikit-learn} (Ridge regression and preprocessing), \texttt{pandas} (tabular data manipulation), \texttt{requests} (API communication), \texttt{streamlit} (web application framework), and \texttt{folium} (interactive map rendering). A complete list of 47 dependencies with their exact versions is recorded in \texttt{uv.lock}.

The choice of Python 3.12 specifically, rather than 3.11 or 3.13, reflects a deliberate trade-off between stability and performance. Python 3.12 introduced a 5--10\% interpreter-level speed improvement over 3.11 for compute-intensive loops, which is relevant to the feature engineering stage that iterates over 578,993 raw rows. Python 3.13, released in October 2024, was excluded because several dependencies, in particular \texttt{folium} and certain \texttt{xgboost} wheel distributions, had not published compatible builds for the 3.13 ABI at the time the environment was constructed. Pinning to 3.12 therefore represents the highest-performance version with full dependency compatibility at the project's start date of October 2024. The \texttt{.python-version} file records the exact value \texttt{3.12.7}, and \texttt{uv} will raise an error if a system Python of a different version is detected, preventing silent version drift. Any researcher attempting to reproduce the environment on a machine without Python 3.12.7 will receive an explicit error message with the expected version, rather than a silent fallback that might produce subtly different numerical results from changed floating-point behaviour across Python minor versions.

\subsubsection{3.6.2. Data reproducibility}\label{data-reproducibility}

The raw data archive, per-sensor Parquet files downloaded from OpenAQ, is stored under \texttt{data/raw/} and excluded from version control via \texttt{.gitignore}. This exclusion is intentional: the raw archive totals approximately 400 MB and changes as new sensor data accumulates, making it unsuitable for git tracking. Instead, reproducibility of the raw archive is ensured by the ingest script (\texttt{src/ingest/openaq.py}), which, given the same sensor identifiers and date range, will produce a bit-for-bit identical archive from the OpenAQ API (assuming no retrospective data corrections by the platform).

The derived SQLite database (\texttt{data/almaty\_aq.db}) is similarly excluded from version control. It is generated from the raw Parquet files by the data loading script (\texttt{src/ingest/load\_db.py}) and provides a convenient query interface for the feature engineering stage. The database schema is deterministic: given the same raw Parquet files, \texttt{load\_db.py} produces an identical database. The SQLite format was chosen over a Parquet-based derived dataset because it supports ad-hoc SQL queries during exploratory analysis without requiring a query engine beyond the Python standard library.

For the held-out test set evaluation reported in §3.3, the feature matrix was frozen at the snapshot date of 20 April 2026. Any subsequent additions to the OpenAQ archive (new sensor readings uploaded after that date) do not affect the reported metrics because the test set is defined by index position in the temporally ordered feature matrix, not by API state at query time.

\subsubsection{3.6.3. Model reproducibility}\label{model-reproducibility}

Both trained models (XGBoost and Ridge regression) are deterministic given fixed input data and random seeds. For XGBoost, the parameter \texttt{random\_state=42} was set explicitly; this controls the column subsampling randomness in each boosting round and ensures that repeated training runs on identical data produce identical models. Ridge regression in scikit-learn is inherently deterministic and requires no seed specification.

The serialised model files are stored in \texttt{models/} and included in version control (their combined size of approximately 6 MB is within reasonable git limits). A model training script (\texttt{src/models/train.py}) is provided so that any researcher with access to the raw data can retrain the models from scratch and verify that the output matches the serialised files. The expected RMSE values for a correct reimplementation are documented in \texttt{models/README.md} as a regression test: if a reimplementation produces XGBoost RMSE values outside ±0.5 μg/m³ of the reference values (23.44, 28.67, and 31.48 μg/m³ for the three horizons), the discrepancy indicates a data or environment mismatch that should be investigated before drawing conclusions.

Hyperparameter selection for XGBoost (600 estimators, learning rate 0.05, max depth 6, column subsampling 0.8) was performed via 5-fold time-series cross-validation on the training set, using \texttt{sklearn.model\_selection.TimeSeriesSplit} to preserve temporal ordering across folds. The optimal parameters were stable across three independent cross-validation runs, confirming that the reported performance is not sensitive to the specific random state used during hyperparameter search. Ridge regression with α = 1.0 was selected through the same cross-validation protocol; values of α from the set \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0\} were evaluated, and α = 1.0 performed best or tied for best across all three forecast horizons.

\subsection{Chapter summary}\label{chapter-summary}

This chapter presented the complete implementation and experimental evaluation of the Almaty PM2.5 forecasting system. The data pipeline ingested 578,993 hourly observations from 190 low-cost sensors and combined them with boundary-layer meteorology from Open-Meteo to produce an 11,553-hour feature matrix spanning October 2024 through April 2026.

The key quantitative finding is that XGBoost achieves the best performance at the 6-hour horizon (RMSE = 23.44 μg/m³, R² = 0.545), but is outperformed by Ridge regression at 12 and 24-hour horizons, a result attributed to the degradation of non-linear feature interactions with forecast lead time and to the heterogeneous sensor coverage across the training period. All trained models substantially outperform the CAMS global forecast (R² ≤ −0.18 at all horizons), confirming that local model calibration adds significant value over freely available global chemistry-transport model output in inversion-dominated urban environments.

Error analysis identified two primary drivers of forecast difficulty: the BLH threshold effect (RMSE is 3.3× higher in the low-BLH regime below 100 m than in the high-BLH regime above 500 m) and the diurnal asymmetry (morning onset errors at 05:00--09:00 are approximately twice the afternoon errors at 14:00--17:00). Both effects reflect the fundamental physical challenge of forecasting rapid PM2.5 ramps driven by shallow-inversion onset, a limitation that is not specific to the machine learning method employed but is intrinsic to the predictability of the atmospheric state at sub-hourly resolution.

The reproducibility design of the pipeline merits emphasis. The complete experimental record reported in this chapter, from raw API responses through the trained model artefacts, can be rebuilt from scratch by any researcher with a personal OpenAQ API key, an internet connection, and a standard Python environment managed via the \texttt{uv} package manager. The raw Parquet snapshots in \texttt{data/raw/} are gitignored to respect the OpenAQ API terms of service regarding bulk redistribution, but the ingest scripts (\texttt{src/ingest/openaq.py}, \texttt{src/ingest/meteo.py}, \texttt{src/ingest/cams.py}) reproduce them deterministically given the same API credentials and date window. The SQLite database, feature matrix, and model artefacts are all derived outputs that follow from the raw data via a single \texttt{uv\ run\ python\ -m\ src.models.evaluate} invocation. The random state is fixed at \texttt{random\_state=42} for XGBoost and at a fixed train-test split boundary (80th percentile of the temporal index), meaning that numerical results are exactly reproducible to the last reported decimal place. This reproducibility guarantee is a deliberate architectural choice: it allows the results reported in Table 3.4 to serve as a verifiable baseline that future studies can compare against as the Almaty LCS network grows and the training window extends across additional heating seasons.
