
\subsection{Motivation and relevance}\label{motivation-and-relevance}

Almaty's winter air pollution is one of the worst urban public-health problems in Central Asia. The city sits in a mountain basin at 700--900 m above sea level, enclosed to the south by the Zailiyskiy Alatau range and open to the steppe to the north. Under anticyclonic winter conditions, a stable temperature inversion forms over the basin, suppressing vertical mixing and trapping combustion products near the surface. During the worst episodes, the atmospheric boundary-layer height (BLH) falls from a summer mean of around 1,000 m to 10--50 m, cutting the effective dilution volume by a factor of 20 to 100. Coal combustion is the dominant emission source in these episodes: most residential heating in peri-urban Almaty runs on solid-fuel boilers, which emit PM2.5 and black carbon at rates that depend strongly on combustion temperature and coal quality. Near-zero ventilation combined with high emission rates drives daily-mean PM2.5 above 100 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ at peak winter, putting the city among the most polluted in the IQAir annual rankings for several consecutive years \cite{iqair_world_2024}. The WHO 24-hour PM2.5 guideline is 15 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ \cite{who_aqg_2021}; Almaty exceeds it by a factor of six or more during inversion events. Monthly means of 80.7 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ (December 2025) and 74.2 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ (January 2026) were recorded in the sensor network used in this study.

Until roughly 2023, the public data infrastructure for monitoring this problem was thin. Almaty's official Kazhydromet network ran fewer than 10 reference-grade stations across a 682 km$^2$ urban area, too sparse to resolve the fine-scale spatial gradients that basin-trapped pollution produces. The rise of community-scale low-cost sensor (LCS) networks changed this. The AirGradient open-hardware platform, combined with the OpenAQ data aggregation API \cite{openaq_platform}, brought a rapid spread of low-cost monitors across the city. As of April 2026, the network used in this study had 192 sensors within a 25 km radius of the city centre (43.24\textdegree{}N, 76.89\textdegree{}E), of which 153 were AirGradient units concentrated in central and southern residential districts. At roughly one sensor per 3.5 km$^2$ on average, with higher density in the most populated zones, this network gives a city-scale spatial picture that the reference network alone could not provide. Other Central Asian cities with similar inversion-driven pollution problems, including Shymkent (Kazakhstan), Karaganda (Kazakhstan), Bishkek (Kyrgyzstan), and Tashkent (Uzbekistan), share the same broad data profile: sparse official monitoring, a growing LCS presence on OpenAQ, and no published machine-learning forecasting work tailored to their geography.

Short-term PM2.5 forecasting is well developed in European and North American settings, where reference-grade training targets and dense meteorological networks are available. Gradient-boosted tree models and long short-term memory networks trained on ground-based or satellite-derived features routinely achieve useful skill at 6--24 h horizons. Almaty falls outside this literature for two reasons. First, data access: the global platforms that publish PM2.5 forecasts, IQAir, AirVisual, and BreezoMeter, run proprietary pipelines whose architectures, training data, and evaluation protocols are not public. Second, institutional capacity: Kazhydromet publishes hourly observations at its stations but has no public forecast API or machine-readable hazard thresholds. No peer-reviewed study has trained a gradient-boosted model on Almaty's LCS network and evaluated it against a freely available calibrated baseline on a held-out winter test period. That gap is the direct motivation for this work.

This work matters beyond Almaty. Central Asia carries one of the highest per-capita PM2.5 disease burdens globally, driven by arid dust sources, coal-heavy energy systems, and fast-growing cities. A reproducible pipeline that needs only an OpenAQ API key, a free Open-Meteo weather archive endpoint \cite{zippenfenig_openmeteo_2023}, and standard Python libraries is directly portable to any LCS-served city in the region. City centre coordinates, API query windows, and training configuration all live in a single configuration file, so researchers or municipal agencies in Bishkek, Shymkent, or Tashkent could adapt the system to their local network in hours, with no proprietary data or institutional data-sharing agreements required.

\subsection{Problem statement}\label{problem-statement}

There is no open, reproducible short-term PM2.5 forecasting system built on Almaty's LCS network, and no published evaluation of how a locally trained model compares to the freely available CAMS global forecast for this geography. The core scientific question is how much predictive skill is gained by training a machine-learning model on Almaty's own sensor data versus applying an off-the-shelf global chemistry-transport model.

Two subsidiary questions follow. First, given the limited LCS dataset (roughly 9,242 training hours across 562 days), it is not clear whether a non-linear gradient-boosted model or a regularised linear model gives better accuracy; the answer has direct implications for model selection in data-scarce cities elsewhere in Central Asia. Second, the spatial and temporal representativeness of a city-median derived from an unevenly distributed LCS network has not been quantified for Almaty. During outage periods the median may reflect fewer than 30 active stations rather than the nominal 192, and what that means for forecast validity is unknown. Answering these questions requires a complete evaluation pipeline running on real, openly accessible data from Almaty's sensor network, not on synthetic or reference-grade surrogates. This study provides that pipeline and reports the first quantitative answers to both questions over a 562-day window ending April 2026.

\subsection{Aim and objectives}\label{aim-and-objectives}

\textbf{Aim.} Develop and evaluate an intelligent short-term PM2.5 forecasting system for Almaty that is built entirely on open data and code and is deployable on free-tier infrastructure.

\textbf{Objectives.} 1. Ingest and curate PM2.5 observations (OpenAQ LCS) and meteorological features (Open-Meteo, including boundary-layer height) for the 2024-10-01 \ensuremath{\rightarrow} 2026-04-15 window into a reproducible SQLite store. 2. Engineer temporal and meteorological features tailored to Almaty's winter inversion regime. 3. Train and cross-validate an XGBoost forecaster for 6/12/24 h horizons and compare it against three baselines: persistence, linear regression, and CAMS. 4. Analyse error regimes - in particular, episodes of sudden onset and rapid clearing - and articulate the model's failure modes. 5. Ship a single-screen Streamlit web application that visualises current observations and the XGBoost forecast on a map of Almaty.

\subsection{Scientific novelty}\label{scientific-novelty}

This study makes three contributions. The first is the initial publicly released PM2.5 forecasting model calibrated for Almaty's LCS network and inversion-dominated winter meteorology. Prior work on Almaty air quality is mostly descriptive (source apportionment, trend analysis) or relies on satellite-derived estimates rather than ground-level sensor data. The model here is trained and evaluated on the 192-station OpenAQ network over a 562-day window ending April 2026, using a feature set that encodes BLH dynamics and inversion-onset signals absent from earlier local work.

The second contribution is the reproducible open architecture. All inputs are openly licensed (CC BY 4.0, no paywalled data); the full pipeline is a single Python repository with no cloud dependencies. Comparable published systems, including those evaluated by \cite{Ayus2023Comparison}{]}, typically rely on proprietary ground truth, restricted API access, or institutional data agreements that block reproduction or geographic transfer. This design removes those barriers, making the pipeline portable to Shymkent, Karaganda, and other Central Asian cities with analogous LCS-on-OpenAQ data profiles.

The third contribution is a concrete result on model-complexity trade-offs. Ridge regression outperforms XGBoost at the 12 and 24-hour horizons: by 9.8\% in RMSE at +12 h (Ridge: 25.85 vs. XGBoost: 28.67 \textmu{}g/m$^3$) and by 13.5\% at +24 h (Ridge: 27.24 vs.~XGBoost: 31.48 \textmu{}g/m$^3$). This result comes from a training set of 9,242 hours with 27 features, a scale at which non-linear ensemble methods would normally be expected to retain their advantage based on published benchmarks. The finding adds to the evidence on model-complexity trade-offs for air quality forecasting with under 10,000 training samples. The most closely related published comparisons \cite{Ayus2023Comparison, mahajan2018shortterm}{]} report similar reversals in settings where the target variable carries substantial measurement noise, consistent with the known hygroscopic overestimation bias in uncalibrated LCS readings.

The reversal also has a direct consequence for system design. Ridge regression needs no tree construction, so its inference time on a single feature row is negligible compared to XGBoost, and its serialised model file is orders of magnitude smaller. On a free-tier Streamlit deployment where memory and compute are tight, a Ridge model at the 12 and 24-hour horizons is not just equally accurate but the better engineering choice. That alignment of statistical and operational considerations makes the case for a multi-model system rather than a single one.

\subsection{Practical significance}\label{practical-significance}

The locally trained XGBoost model achieves RMSE = 23.44 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ and R$^2$ = 0.545 at the 6-hour forecast horizon on a held-out winter test set of 2,311 hours (December 2025 -- April 2026), outperforming the CAMS global chemistry-transport model by 41.1\% in RMSE at the same horizon. At the 12-hour horizon, Ridge regression produces RMSE = 25.85 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ and R$^2$ = 0.446, outperforming CAMS by approximately 28\% in RMSE. At 24 hours, Ridge achieves RMSE = 27.24 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ and R$^2$ = 0.383, while CAMS produces negative R$^2$ values at all three horizons (--0.314, --0.376, and --0.184 at 6, 12, and 24 h respectively), indicating that the global model provides no useful predictive signal for this geography. All locally trained models outperform CAMS by at least 16\% in RMSE across every horizon tested.

These results are deployed as a Streamlit web application that provides a continuously updated PM2.5 forecast for Almaty at no cost. The application refreshes every 30--60 seconds, requires no authentication, and runs in any browser, making it usable by schools, hospitals, municipal public health offices, and community organisations without technical staff or data infrastructure. Forecast uncertainty is shown as a confidence band of roughly \ensuremath{\pm}17 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ at the 6-hour horizon (\ensuremath{\pm}1 MAE). An advisory triggered when the lower bound crosses the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 \textmu{}g/m$^3$ will catch genuine deterioration events at a false-positive rate acceptable for public communication. The map view shows the current city-median PM2.5 alongside the three horizon forecasts; the colour scale follows Kazakhstani national AQI categories so non-specialist users can read the values without knowing \textmu{}g/m$^3$ numerics. The full pipeline, covering data ingest, feature engineering, model training, and the application, is a single open Python repository with no cloud dependencies and is directly portable to other Central Asian cities.

\subsection{Structure of the thesis}\label{structure-of-the-thesis}

Chapter 1 reviews the scientific and policy context: the meteorological and emission drivers of Almaty's PM2.5 episodes (temperature inversions, coal combustion); the low-cost sensor technology and the OpenAQ data ecosystem behind this dataset; and the methodological literature on gradient-boosted tree models for short-term urban PM2.5 forecasting, with emphasis on inversion-dominated mountain or basin environments. Chapter 2 describes the data sources and system architecture in reproducible detail, covering the SQLite database schema, the OpenAQ and Open-Meteo API ingestion procedures, the 27-feature matrix (BLH delta, temporal lag, and calendar features), the train/test split, and the rationale for each non-obvious design choice. Chapter 3 reports the experimental results: quantitative model comparisons across all three forecast horizons and all four models, feature importance, error-regime case studies on inversion onset and clearing events, and the spatial analysis of sensor-coverage effects during the October--November 2025 AirGradient firmware outage. Chapter 4 discusses what the results mean for the stated research questions, places the findings within the published literature, quantifies the main limitations, and proposes five directions for future work, including SHAP-based feature attribution, forecast BLH integration, and an XGBoost--Ridge ensemble. An appendix gives the full model hyperparameter configurations, API query specifications, and data-quality filter thresholds applied during ingestion, so the complete pipeline can be reproduced from the archived code and database.
